Approach

Why it is built this way

The Science on Screen talk sorts the house into five experiments: the foundation, exterior insulation, no drywall, controlled ventilation, and solar on the walls. This page is the reason for each choice. What happened on site, including the wind, is in the journal.

A shallow foundation, not a basement

The first question was whether the house needed a basement. Digging one added excavation, concrete, moisture, and work that is hard to do alone. The alternative in the build is a frost-protected shallow foundation: insulation keeps the soil from freezing, instead of relying on depth alone.

The photographs show Fox Blocks insulated concrete forms in that foundation. This is not a house with no foundation. It is a house that skipped the basement.

Insulation outside the structure

The thermal idea is straightforward. Put almost all of the insulation outside the frame, so the structure sits inside the coat. The layer he describes is about 6 inches of EPS and 1 inch of polyiso, on the order of R-33.

The experiment on site was less about the R-value than about keeping large sheets of foam in place in the wind, then covering them with the wood siding.

Wood, including wood fiber

Cladding, interior panels, and much of the lumber are wood, including local pine and other domestic stock. Wood fiber from Maine is in the material record as a batt. It sits beside the foam exterior, and it does not replace that foam in the account of this house.

The reason for the wood is repair. A finish that can come off can be fixed, changed, or opened without starting from demolition.

Air, on purpose

Skipping drywall is one of the experiments. A tight shell is the other half: fresh air has to be brought in on purpose. The house uses an energy-recovery ventilator, which exchanges heat and moisture between the outgoing air and the air coming in.

Through spring, summer, and fall so far, the indoor air has held near 55 percent relative humidity and about 70–75°F. Winter is the season that has not been the test yet. There is no long chart of humidity and temperature on this site. The summary, and what is still missing, is on the data page.

All-electric, with the panels on the walls

The operating aim is an all-electric house with low demand, and generation on the building. That is a goal for how the house should run. It is not a claim that every fossil-fuel use is already gone, or that the house has produced more energy than it has used.

The panels are vertical, on the walls, and they also stand in for some of the cladding. The bet is winter. A roof under snow can sit idle, while a vertical wall sheds snow and faces the low sun. He reports that reflection off snow has briefly taken the wall array to about 110 percent of its rated output. That is one observed peak, not a measured season.

Annual comparisons are model estimates, not meter readings from Franconia. They are written up, with the assumptions, on the data page.

Able to be changed

Renovations taught a specific dislike: walls that have to be destroyed before anyone can see what is in them. The interior wood, and the decision to skip drywall, are there so the next change can start by taking a finish off. He notes that the next person could be him.

That is the adaptability this site can describe. It does not document a claim that wiring and plumbing can be reworked without touching anything else.
