What the numbers are, and are not

Two kinds of figures belong here. One is a short record of indoor conditions. The other is a solar model used to compare wall panels with a roof. They are not the same kind of evidence.

Indoor humidity and temperature

With the energy-recovery ventilator running, spring, summer, and fall so far have held near 55 percent relative humidity and about 70–75°F. That is the summary from the build record. It is not a full year, and winter is the season still ahead.

There is no time series of humidity or temperature on this site yet. When a longer record exists, it can sit here beside this note.

A solar model, not the meter

The charts compare two configurations at a reference location of 45° N, 71.75° W. That point follows the latitude assumption used for the talk. It is north of Franconia, and it is not a survey of this roof or these trees.

The roof line is a comparison case: 32 panels at 415 watts, nearly flat, tilted about 2 to 2.5 degrees toward the west. It is not a description of panels built on this house. The wall line is the arrangement under study: 11 panels at 415 watts on each of the vertical east, south, and west walls. The model is PVGIS 5.2, with a 14 percent system-loss assumption. It does not include the tree line, snow cover, batteries, inverter limits, or what the house actually uses.

In that model the roof reference is about 13.0 MWh a year. The three walls together are about 9.1 MWh, roughly 30 percent less. The south wall is about 3.7 MWh; the east and west walls are about 2.7 MWh each. In November, December, and January the walls produce more than the roof reference, even before any snow loss is imagined. Winter, December through February, is about 11 percent of the roof year and about 18 percent of the wall year.

A separate snow scenario sets the roof to zero for January through March. That is an assumption, not a PVGIS snow estimate and not a measurement. Under it, the roof year falls to about 10.8 MWh, and the walls finish about 16 percent lower than that reduced roof, while still producing through the months the roof is assumed to be covered.

Snow reflection is a different observation. Daniel reports that during a bright, snowy stretch the wall array briefly reached about 110 percent of rated power. The seasonal energy from that effect has not been quantified, and it is not in the charts.
